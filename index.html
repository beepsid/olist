Starting point

This dataset was not plug and play

The Olist dataset is one of the most popular public e-commerce datasets on Kaggle and most tutorials treat it like it's clean out of the box. It isn't. Before a single chart could be built, the data needed a real audit. Here's a sample of what turned up:

  • 01 Broken category-translation join — 2 Portuguese category names had no English match in the translation table, silently producing null categories for real products.
  • 02 261,831 duplicate rows in the geolocation table (out of 1M+ rows) — left unchecked, these inflate map visuals and slow down the model.
  • 03 Zip codes silently truncated — leading zeros were being dropped when the column was read as a number, causing every zip starting with 0 to silently fail on any geo join.
  • 04 551 orders with conflicting reviews — duplicate review entries for the same order, making average-score calculations unreliable without deduplication.
  • 05 No direct relationship between customers/sellers and geography — required building role-playing dimension tables to handle Power BI's single-active-relationship constraint.

The full audit; 14 issues, every decision, every reason, is documented below.

Data Cleaning & Modeling

Every issue. Every decision. Every reason.

The goal was to clean without fabricating data(no revenue rows dropped), no values invented without a defensible strategy. Click any row to see the full decision.

Issue Found

610 products had no category name in the translation table.

Decision Made

Bucketed as "Uncategorized" rather than dropped.

Why

These rows still had real revenue data. Dropping them would understate total sales figures.

Issue Found

2 products were missing all physical dimension attributes (weight, length, height, width).

Decision Made

Dropped both rows.

Why

2 out of 33,000+ products — statistically immaterial. No category context to impute from reliably.

Issue Found

4 products had weight_g = 0, which is physically impossible and would corrupt freight calculations.

Decision Made

Imputed using the median weight of all other products in the same category.

Why

Category-specific imputation avoids nonsensical cross-category fallbacks. A phone case and a couch should not share a median.

Issue Found

A subset of orders had null approval and/or delivery date columns.

Decision Made

Left as null. No imputation applied.

Why

These are predominantly canceled or undelivered orders. They legitimately never got an approval or delivery timestamp — filling them would fabricate data.

Issue Found

775 order IDs existed in the orders table but had no corresponding rows in the order items table.

Decision Made

Kept in the orders fact table, but explicitly excluded from revenue measures.

Why

Preserves accurate cancellation-rate reporting. Dropping them entirely would understate how often orders fail to result in a sale.

Issue Found

814 duplicate review IDs — 551 orders had 2 or more review entries, making average-score calculations unreliable.

Decision Made

Deduplicated by sorting on review creation date, then removing duplicates. Kept the latest review per order.

Why

The latest review reflects the customer's final, settled sentiment — more meaningful than an earlier reaction.

Issue Found

9 payment rows had a value of zero or negative.

Decision Made

Kept in the table but flagged.

Why

Cross-referencing payment_type suggested these are likely voucher-only orders where the discount covered the full price. Dropping them removes valid order events.

Issue Found

2 credit card payments showed 0 installments, which is logically inconsistent.

Decision Made

Left as-is rather than imputing a value.

Why

Imputing "1 installment" is an assumption. With only 2 rows, the risk of fabricating incorrect installment data outweighs the benefit of a clean count.

Issue Found

3 payment rows had a payment_type value of "not_defined".

Decision Made

Recoded to "Other".

Why

Enables clean categorical grouping in visuals without creating a confusing "not_defined" slice in charts.

Issue Found

Zip codes in the customers, sellers, and geolocation tables were being read as integers, silently dropping the leading zero from any zip starting with 0.

Decision Made

Re-cast all zip code columns as text, then zero-padded to 5 digits using Power Query's Text.PadStart.

Why

Without this fix, any zip beginning with 0 silently fails on geo joins — producing nulls in customer/seller location lookups without any error message.

Issue Found

Product category names had inconsistent casing and formatting. 2 Portuguese categories had no English equivalent in the translation table.

Decision Made

Standardized all names via Power Query. Manually added the 2 missing mappings to the translation table.

Why

Ensures every product resolves to a real, readable English category — no "null" slices in category-level charts.

Issue Found

The geolocation table had 1M+ rows with 261,831 exact duplicates — one zip code could have hundreds of nearly-identical coordinate entries.

Decision Made

Deduplicated, then grouped by zip code prefix and computed centroid (mean lat/lng per zip). Reduces table to ~19K clean rows.

Why

A centroid is a defensible approximation at city/state map zoom level. The alternative (keeping 1M rows with duplicates) inflates the model and corrupts any coordinate averaging done in DAX.

Issue Found

31 lat/lng coordinates fell outside Brazil's geographic bounding box — likely data entry errors.

Decision Made

Filtered out before the centroid aggregation step, not after.

Why

If these outlier points were included in the mean, they would skew the centroid for their zip code — placing a valid zip's map pin in the wrong location.

Issue Found

The geo table needed to serve both customer location and seller location, but Power BI only allows one active relationship from a single dimension table.

Decision Made

Duplicated the cleaned geo table into customer_geo and seller_geo via Power Query References (not copies). Each has its own active relationship.

Why

Power Query References mean the geo cleaning logic only exists once — both tables stay in sync. Avoids Power BI's single-active-relationship-path limitation without workarounds like USERELATIONSHIP everywhere.

Supporting technical details
Built a custom Calendar table in DAX using MIN() and MAX() of dates from order table with Year, Quarter, Month, MonthName, YearMonth, and a numeric YearMonthSort column — because text-based YearMonth sorts alphabetically by default, which breaks time-series charts.

Added multiple inactive relationships from the Calendar table to secondary date columns (delivered date, review date) for future USERELATIONSHIP() time-intelligence measures.

Diagnosed and fixed a cross-filter direction bug: a Single-direction relationship between orders and reviews was causing the "Avg Delivery Delay by Review Score" chart to show the same overall average in every bar. Fixed by setting the relationship to Both.

Data Model

Star schema — clean, unambiguous, fast

Two fact tables connected to six dimension tables, with role-playing geo dimensions solving Power BI's single-active-relationship constraint.

Fact Tables

  • fact_orders — order-level metrics & timestamps
  • fact_order_items — line-item revenue & freight

Dimension Tables

  • dim_products — attributes & English categories
  • dim_customers — customer location
  • dim_sellers — seller location
  • dim_customer_geo — role-playing geo
  • dim_seller_geo — role-playing geo
  • dim_calendar — custom DAX date table

Key Findings

What the data actually says

Delivery timing drives review sentiment

Orders arriving with little or no buffer ahead of the estimated delivery date skewed toward significantly worse review scores — even before counting outright-late deliveries. On-time isn't enough; customers notice when it's barely on-time.

Supply is concentrated; demand is spread

Seller supply is heavily concentrated in São Paulo, Paraná, and Minas Gerais, while customer demand is distributed much more evenly across all states — a real geographic supply-vs-demand imbalance visible in the choropleth maps.

78% credit card · spike at exactly 10 installments

~78% of all payments use credit card, with a pronounced spike at exactly 10 installments. This is consistent with Brazil's common "10x sem juros" (interest-free in 10) retail financing pattern — visible as a sharp outlier in the installment distribution chart.

Seasonal revenue spike — Nov 2017

Revenue shows clear seasonality, with a pronounced peak around November 2017 — matching Brazil's Black Friday and holiday season ramp-up. Month-over-month and rolling 3-month measures make the pattern unambiguous.
